Detectors and distance live in spacetime

An instantaneous stabilizer matrix describes which Pauli operators are known at one time. A noisy Floquet memory is a history of parity measurements. Distance must therefore distinguish harmless changes of that history from undetectable histories that act logically.

From random outcomes to a detector

An edge measurement outcome is not itself a syndrome bit. Even without physical noise, measuring an operator outside the current ISG can produce a fresh random sign. The useful invariant is a plaquette product assembled from edge outcomes in the rounds where its factors were measured.

Let s_p(t) be the inferred sign of plaquette p after the required outcomes have become available. A detector compares compatible inferences, for example

D_p(t) = s_p(t) s_p(t-Delta).

In an ideal history, D_p(t)=+1. A fault can flip a collection of nearby detectors. The exact delay Delta, which outcomes enter s_p, and the startup/readout rules belong to the measurement circuit. They are not stored in the static CheckMatrix.

A Fault-Tolerant Honeycomb Memory constructs the periodic detector graphs and correlation-aware matching problem for explicit circuits. The Atlas schedule export stops one layer earlier: it gives the ideal measured Paulis and their order.

Why snapshot distance answers the wrong question

Suppose a Pauli error has low weight on one time slice. Three cases are possible:

  1. it creates detectors and is therefore visible;
  2. it can be absorbed into changes of measurement outcomes or stabilizer representatives without changing encoded information;
  3. it creates no detector but transports or flips a logical class.

Only the third case is a logical fault. A minimum-weight normalizer element of one snapshot does not account for faulty measurements, errors extended over several rounds, or equivalences that move faults through the measurement history.

Blackwell and Haah formalize this distinction in The Code Distance of Floquet Codes. Their distance minimizes the weight of an undetectable non-benign spacetime error. Benign errors form the equivalence class that neither changes detector data nor acts on the encoded information.

The current Atlas engine recomputes the first two. It has no generic spacetime benign-error quotient, so the third remains an honest gap. Recording a static exact distance under property d would attach machine certainty to the wrong object.

What a replayable fault-distance artifact needs

A future verifier must receive at least:

  1. preparation and final-readout conventions;
  2. the physical measurement circuit for every ideal parity operator;
  3. a named fault alphabet and location weights;
  4. detector definitions, including temporal boundaries;
  5. logical observables in the chosen input/output frames;
  6. benign generators or an equivalent quotient rule;
  7. an exact or bounded search with a replayable witness.

The result should be keyed by circuit and observation window, not placed as a family-wide scalar on the honeycomb check matrix. This missing engine is tracked by the project's Floquet fault-distance Wanted item.

Decoder consequence

The instantaneous toric map is still useful: it predicts topological detector structure and helps identify logical homology classes. It does not remove the time coordinate. A matching graph for the honeycomb memory includes spacelike and timelike edges, correlations from two-qubit measurements, and special nodes at boundaries. Rebuilding that graph after a circuit change is part of the code definition at the fault-tolerant level.
